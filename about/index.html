About SpeedLab

SpeedLab is a set of short, sharp browser tests for click speed, reaction time, memory, and typing — the kind of thing you open to settle an argument and close ten minutes later, one more try in.

Why we built it

SpeedLab started with a simple frustration: most "test your reflexes" sites bury a two-second game under pop-ups, sign-up walls, autoplay sound, and a loading screen that takes longer than the test itself. We wanted the opposite — open a page, and the test is right there, playable in under a second, on a phone or a laptop, with nothing to install and nobody asking for your email. Everything on the site is built around that one rule.

It grew from there into a small collection that now spans clicking, reaction and timing, keyboard speed, memory, and quick arcade games, with new tests and written guides added regularly. Each one is designed to be the sort of thing you open to settle an argument and close ten minutes later, one more try in.

What's on the site

  • Mouse & clicking — click speed (CPS), tap speed, right-click and a plain click counter.
  • Reaction & timing — visual and audio reaction time, an aim trainer, whack-a-mole, Go/No-Go, stop-the-clock and an endless runner.
  • Keyboard — typing speed (WPM), key mashing, alphabet and number sprints, and type-a-word-fast.
  • Memory & brain — number, sequence and visual memory, the chimp test, mental math, word puzzles and 2048.
  • Guides — plain-English articles explaining what each metric means, what a good score is, and how to actually improve, from average click speed to training your reaction time.

How scoring and ranks work

Every test turns your result into a number and then into one of seven ranks — from Snail up to Lightning — so a bare score has context. Those ranks and the "faster than X% of people" line are based on typical score distributions for each test: the reaction figures sit around the well-documented 200–250 ms human average, the CPS bands around the usual 6–7 clicks-per-second, typing around the ~40 WPM average, and so on. They're honest reference ranges to show where you land, not a scientific instrument — your exact number also depends on your device, screen and how warmed up you are. Where it matters, like reaction time, we average several attempts rather than trusting a single lucky tap, because one try is too noisy to mean anything.

How it's built

The whole site is plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript — no frameworks, no build step, no external services. Fonts are hosted here, so nothing loads from anywhere else. Once a page has loaded once, it works offline. Your scores and settings live in your browser's local storage and go no further; there are no accounts, no tracking pixels, no analytics, and no cookies. The only third-party code is the advertising that keeps the site free, and even that loads nothing until the page is ready.

Who it's for

Anyone curious how fast they really are — gamers warming up before a match, people comparing scores with friends, students on a break, or someone who just wants to know if their reaction time is any good. Because sound is off by default and there's nothing to sign up for, it's safe to open on a shared or school computer.
